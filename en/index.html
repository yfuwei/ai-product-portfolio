01 / Product Judgments

Five Unpopular Takes on AI Products:

01｜The biggest problem for customer-service AI may have nothing to do with customer service.

The problem is not that customer service answered poorly. It is that the company treated a reply as a resolution.

A customer follows up on a refund for the third time. For the third time, AI explains the refund policy accurately, politely, and in full. Yet by Friday, there is still no traceable task for the warehouse check promised on Wednesday.

Rather than polishing customer-service scripts, P2 lays out a service continuation workflow: recover past commitments, find the unfinished work for the same return, hand it to an Agent Workflow to advance the warehouse check, then continue refund processing based on the business result.

WH-201 Waiting means it is still waiting. RF-301 Started does not mean the refund is complete. AI taking part in the process is no excuse to dress up task progress as a business outcome.

Customers do not need a robot that apologizes better. They need someone to keep working until the issue is resolved.

02｜Hallucinations may not be RAG's biggest problem.

The most deceptive error is not AI inventing evidence. It is AI using real evidence to make the wrong claim.

An expired policy can be authentic. A historical case can have an accurate source. The more polished the citations, the more likely users are to trust a conclusion that does not apply to the current business situation at all.

P1's retrieval tests exposed a similar risk: historical material could rank highly because it was semantically closer to the question. Instead of adding Rerank first, I adjusted Retrieval Scope and Metadata to distinguish valid policies, regional rules, and historical references.

I then designed a separate Eligibility check to determine whether the evidence actually applies to the current task.

I would rather AI tell users plainly, “This source cannot be used for the current project,” than give them wrong advice with perfect citations.

03｜Multi-Agent isn't always an architectural upgrade. Sometimes it just dilutes accountability.

Each Agent completing its own work does not mean someone owns the customer's issue through to resolution.

The warehouse check takes three days. Business conditions may change in the meantime. When the event arrives, who verifies things again? Who decides to continue refund processing? Who makes sure no duplicate task is created?

Warehouse Agent checks the warehouse. Refund Agent checks the refund. Both Agents complete their own queries.

P2's design gives the Service Orchestrator ownership of the ongoing service goal, waiting state, and plan version. A warehouse event does not itself mean the check is complete. Once the wait ends, Refund Agent must query existing tasks again before deciding whether to continue one or create a new one.

But this still does not prove that three Agents are the best architecture.

If one Workflow and two tools can do this more reliably, I will cut the extra Agents without hesitation.

Multi-Agent must prove its value through task outcomes, not the number of boxes on an architecture diagram.

04｜A full enterprise ontology may just be an expensive vanity project.

Before the AI product has proved it is worth building, the infrastructure supporting it has become the biggest project.

AI needs to understand the relationships between policy versions, regional rules, and historical cases. So we start standardizing terminology, defining a complete Schema, building a domain Ontology, and planning an enterprise Knowledge Graph.

It sounds visionary.

But a few critical business relationships can turn into a long-term knowledge infrastructure project.

Before the AI product has proved it is worth building, the infrastructure supporting it has become the biggest project.

P1 ultimately chooses to keep only the minimal Knowledge Graph relationship layer needed for the current task, stop the plan for a complete Domain Ontology, and choose to integrate established Wiki capabilities.

Not every potentially useful capability is worth building again ourselves.

I am not against building foundations. I refuse to let the foundations swallow the product.

05｜Human-in-the-loop can also be an excuse for product teams to dodge accountability.

Instead of automation, the company gets another queue of approvals.

Worried AI might judge incorrectly? Add a human confirmation step. Worried Agent execution might get out of control? Add another approval. The system cannot handle an exception? Send everything to a human.

In the end, AI generates suggestions while employees check every item, click again and again, and remain accountable for clicking the wrong button.

Instead of automation, the company gets another queue of approvals.

In P2's proposed workflow, read-only rechecks after a warehouse event, valid-record verification, and checks for existing tasks should be handled by tools and explicit rules wherever possible. Only conflicting evidence, insufficient authorization, or high-impact business actions should be escalated to humans.

The same applies to P1. If evidence is not eligible for use, the system should refuse to draw a definitive conclusion, rather than throw an unverified judgment at an employee and ask them to “just confirm it.”

If a human review cannot name the error it is supposed to catch, it is not governance. It is passing the responsibility on.
