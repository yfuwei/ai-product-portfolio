Failure Diagnosis

Built is not the same as usable.
AI projects often fail in three places.

01 False Demand

Mistaking model capability
for business demand.

First determine whether this is a real business problem or just a demonstration of what the model can do.

02 False Result

Mistaking AI output
for business outcome.

If the output cannot be confirmed, routed, tracked, or reviewed, it is not yet business capability.

03 False Efficiency

Mistaking unclear automation
for efficiency.

Automation without responsibility boundaries only packages risk as speed.

Scenario Discovery

Not every business problem
deserves to be carried by AI.

I do not first ask, “Can this be done with AI?” I first ask whether the business has a repeated, costly, and verifiable problem.

A strong AI scenario is not where a model is easiest to connect. It is where AI intervention can change a decision, action, or result.

01

A request is not the same as a valid AI scenario

Is this request truly worth solving with AI?

Not every stated need should become an AI project. I look at frequency, importance, verifiability, and whether AI can change the business result.

02

Generation is not the same as adoption

Will this output actually be used by the business?

A model can write, summarize, or classify, but a scenario only exists when there is a clear user, next action, workflow entry point, and result validation.

User Next Action Workflow Entry Result Validation
03

Automation is not the same as responsibility transfer

Should this process really be automated?

The question is not whether AI can automate something, but which step AI should handle and where human confirmation and responsibility boundaries must remain.

Key judgment: A key ability of an AI PM is to stop the wrong AI project from being launched.

This is not another AI project document. It defines gates across scoping, pilot, launch, and iteration so that risky AI projects are not pushed forward too quickly.

Differentiated Positioning

My differentiation is not that I know how to put AI on a page.
It is knowing where AI should enter — and where it should stop.

An AI product manager does not only push projects toward approval; they also stop the wrong ones from being approved.

I am best suited for teams that already see an AI opportunity, but have not yet clarified what to build, how to build it, and where to stop.

I do not want to own the stage of “making AI work” alone. I want to help AI move from demo to business, from capability to clear responsibility.

01

The scenario is not yet clear

Not every business problem should be translated into AI.

I first identify which problems are frequent, important, verifiable, and worth AI intervention.

02

The workflow cannot yet catch the output

The goal is not to generate more AI output.

The goal is to make sure the output can be confirmed, routed, tracked, and reviewed.

03

The boundary is not yet clear

AI should not automate everything.

I define where AI should assist, where humans must confirm, and where responsibility remains.

Background

I have not been focused on a single industry.
I have been focused on how complex decisions can be strengthened by products.

Architecture, real estate product management, and AI applications may look like different stages. But the question I kept working on is consistent: how people make decisions in complex contexts, how organizations turn decisions into processes, and how products make judgment more stable and reusable.

01

ArchitectureDecisions in Space

Architectural training taught me to think from scenes, constraints, users, and long-term outcomes. A spatial solution is not about placing elements into a plan. It is about understanding how people act, choose, and coordinate within it.

02

VankeDecisions in Organizations

In regional product management, I worked with product decisions across departments, cities, and roles: positioning, standards, reviews, trade-offs, and execution. This made me care more about workflow, responsibility boundaries, and organizational coordination.

03

AIDecisions with AI Involved

In AI applications, I focus less on what the model can do and more on how AI enters real business: when it should assist judgment, when it should enter a workflow, and when it must stop before human confirmation.

The industry changed. The question did not: how to make complex judgment visible, organized, and productized.

Judgment Notes

A few reminders I keep bringing into AI product work.

These are not slogans. They are reminders I use to calibrate direction when judging AI scenarios, designing products, and validating implementation.

01

Not every AI product should become a chatbot.

Many scenarios do not need a conversational interface. They need embedded judgment, routing, reminders, and confirmation inside the workflow.

02

AI Coding makes the right direction faster — and the wrong direction faster too.

AI can speed up implementation, but unclear scenarios, boundaries, and validation can also scale the wrong direction faster.

03

“Build first, justify later” is where many AI projects begin to fail.

AI projects can be piloted in small steps, but they should not skip scenario judgment, responsibility boundaries, and result validation.

04

AI can replace tasks, but it should not easily replace responsibility.

The more valuable the scenario, the more clearly we need to define who confirms, who owns the fallback, and how mistakes are rolled back.

05

An AI product manager should not only push projects forward.

They should also stop the wrong AI projects from being launched.
