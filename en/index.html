METHOD | AI Product Judgment

Five product judgments stand between AI opportunity and business usability.

Three failure modes often keep a built AI solution out of the business.

01False Demand

Treating model capability as a business need.

First determine whether this is a real business problem or merely a capability demo.

02False Outcome

Treating AI output as a business result.

If the output cannot be confirmed, routed, tracked, and reviewed, it is not yet a business capability.

03False Efficiency

Treating automation without clear accountability as efficiency.

Who confirms, who owns the risk, and how errors are rolled back must be defined by the product first.

  1. 01

    Scenario Judgment

    Demand does not mean AI is worth doing.

    Is this problem actually worth AI intervention?

    Look at whether the problem is real, frequent and important, verifiable, and whether AI can change the business outcome.

    Not every business problem deserves AI.

  2. 02

    Task Judgment

    Generative ability does not equal a use case.

    Which step of the task should AI actually carry?

    Look for a clear user, required context, next action, the workflow the output enters, and how the result will be validated.

  3. 03

    Responsibility Judgment

    Automatable does not mean it should be automated.

    What should AI own, and what must remain human-owned?

    Define Human Review and confirmation, accountability, fallback, rollback, and final responsibility in advance.

  4. 04

    Capability Boundary

    More mature capabilities do not mean the product should build everything itself.

    What should we still build, what should we integrate, what must we retain, and what should we stop rebuilding?

    Based on current task responsibility and mature external capabilities, continuously make Build / Integrate / Retain / Stop decisions.

    Project 1: Retain Raw Evidence and the minimal Knowledge Graph relationship layer, stop building a full Domain Ontology, and integrate mature Wiki capabilities.

  5. 05

    Validation Loop

    A running demo does not mean the direction is right.

    How do we know whether to continue, pause, or rethink?

    Use evaluation metrics, Bad Cases, version records, and regression tests to turn failures into product rules for the next iteration.

BACKGROUND | Where the Judgment Comes From

My focus has never been one industry, but how products can strengthen complex decision-making.

One Consistent Judgment Thread

Architecture, real-estate product management, and AI applications may look like different stages, but the same question runs through them: how people make judgments in complex situations, how organizations turn judgment into process, and how products make those judgments more stable and reusable.

01 Architecture Stage

Decisions in Space

Architecture trained me to start with context, constraints, users, and long-term outcomes. Designing a space means judging how people will act, choose, and collaborate—not simply arranging elements.

02 Vanke Stage

Decisions in Organizations

Regional product management involved judgments across departments, cities, and roles: positioning, standards, reviews, delivery, and trade-offs. It made me focus on process, accountability, and organizational coordination.

03 AI Stage

Decisions with AI in the Loop

In AI applications, I focus on how AI enters real business, beyond what the model can do: when it should support judgment, enter a workflow, or stop for human confirmation.

Industries change, but the question does not: how can complex judgment be made visible, organized, and productized?

POSITIONING | Where I Fit Best

My differentiation is not putting AI on a page, but judging where AI should enter—and where it should stop.

An AI Product Manager is not only responsible for launching projects, but also for stopping the wrong ones.

I fit best when the business already sees an AI opportunity, but has not yet decided what to build, how to build it, or where to stop.

JUDGMENT NOTES

  1. 01

    Not every AI product should become a chatbox.

    Many scenarios do not need a conversational interface; they need judgment, routing, reminders, and confirmation embedded in the workflow.

  2. 02

    AI Coding makes the right thing faster—and the wrong thing faster.

    AI can accelerate validation, but it cannot replace judgment about the scenario, boundaries, and outcomes.

  3. 03

    AI can replace tasks, but it should not casually replace responsibility.

    The higher the stakes, the more explicitly the product must define who confirms, who owns the risk, and how errors are rolled back.
